## w01_premise_scene

**Task:** Write a 600-800 word scene. Premise: a lighthouse keeper on a remote island receives a letter, postmarked yesterday, from her brother, who drowned eleven years ago. Show, don't explain. End the scene before the mystery is solved. Return only the scene.

### A

The wind hammered the glass of the lantern room like a drummer's fist, a relentless rhythm that had kept the island’s horizon steady for the last decade. Mara stood at the rail, her fingers tightening around the brass compass she’d carried since the storm that took the old keeper’s life. The light slid obediently along the curved wall, painting the stone with a pale, steady glow.

Her hand slipped, and she let the compass fall, clattering against the cold metal floor. The sound seemed absurdly loud in the stillness of the night, a reminder that even the smallest noises here were magnified by isolation. She wiped her palm on her jeans and turned back to the window, watching the sea churn like a living beast.

The bell of the lighthouse chimed softly, a low, resonant note that vibrated through the walls. Mara turned her head, heart thudding, and saw the wooden door at the base of the tower creak open. The smell of salt and damp wood rolled in, and a figure appeared in the doorway.

“Good evening, Mara,” the voice said. It was quiet, but it carried the timbre of her brother, Dan—his voice, once a steady, booming presence that had steadied her when the waves grew too high. She turned to look at him, the face she had never seen since he had drowned eleven years ago.

She felt her throat tighten, the memories of that night coming back in shards. The sea had been a black, hungry beast that had swallowed him whole, and the only thing left was a battered wooden boat with a single, white flag flapping in the wind. She remembered the way Dan had laughed as they tried to salvage a piece of his life, his eyes bright with the promise of adventure.

But here he was, in the doorway, his hair slicked back, his eyes a deep, familiar blue. He was alive, and the sea that had taken him seemed to have forgotten it.

“Dan?” she whispered. “I thought—”

He held up a small envelope, its seal still intact. The paper smelled of the sea and something else, something dry, like the air after a storm. He pressed it into her palm. The postmark was fresh, the ink still soft, marked with a date: Yesterday.

Mara stared at the envelope. She knew the postmark would be impossible—she had been alone for eleven years. The only way letters could reach her was from a ship or a passerby, but none had come. Her heart hammered, and she felt a cold sweat form at her temples.

She turned to the window, the light’s beam cutting through the fog. The waves lapped against the rocks, each crash a reminder of the relentless passage of time. Her mind tried to reconcile the impossible: the letter, the postmark, the impossible presence of Dan. She felt a knot tighten in her chest.

The envelope was warm to the touch, as if it had been held in a hand for a moment. She could see faint outlines on the paper—her brother’s handwriting, unmistakable, a looping, calligraphic scrawl that had always made her smile. The words, when she finally read them, were simple, but they carried a weight that pressed on her chest like a stone.

“Dear Mara,” the letter began. “I know this is crazy. I know I died here, and I know that I’ve been gone for years. But the sea, it’s never let me rest. I’m writing to you because I’ve been hearing the wind talk. It told me I should bring this to you.” The letter continued, a story of a ship that had passed the island, a single, weary sailor who had found a way to reach her, and a promise that he had hidden something for her to find. The letter ended abruptly, the last sentence cut short, as if the pen had been stopped in the middle of a thought.

Mara closed her eyes, trying to find the rhythm of Dan’s voice in the line. She could hear the sea in the words—crashing, rolling, a vast, unending expanse. She could hear the wind’s sigh in the punctuation.

She opened the envelope. Inside, folded neatly, was a small, weathered map of the island, drawn by a hand she recognized from her childhood drawings. The map was labeled with a single line: “The Lighthouse – The Cave.” The map was not a modern one; it was hand-drawn, with ink that had bled into the paper, giving it an antiquated, almost mystical feel. Mara traced the line with her finger, feeling the grooves of the paper beneath her skin.

The letter had ended abruptly, but there was a hint of a new line in the bottom left corner: “Find what you’re looking for. Trust the wind.” The words were written in Dan’s hand, but the ink was darker, as if someone had pressed it harder. Mara felt a chill run down her spine.

The wind outside seemed to pause, as though it listened too. The light in the lantern flickered for a moment, then steadied again. She heard her own breathing, loud in the quiet of the tower, a steady drumbeat.

She stared at the map, feeling the weight of it against her palm. The paper was damp, as if it had been in the sea, and the ink had a faint saltiness that made her think of the tide. She thought of the day Dan had disappeared, of the waves that had claimed him, and of the way the sea had always seemed to have a presence of its own.

A knock echoed in the stone corridor, sudden and sharp. Mara's heart leaped. She turned to the door, half expecting the wind to have somehow found a way to knock on a door. She opened it, and the hallway was empty, the only sound the low, rhythmic thrum of the light.

Her hand moved on instinct, fingers tightening around the compass, the same one that had guided her through nights of endless waves. The wind pressed against the glass, a low, constant hum, and the letter’s words seemed to echo back to her.

She stared at the map again, feeling the pull of the tide in her bones, the unspoken promise that the sea had whispered to her. She knew she was not alone, that the sea had somehow carried something—someone—back to her. The mystery lay not in whether Dan was dead or alive, but in what he had left for her, and why the sea had decided to bring it back.

The wind’s sigh deepened, and the light in the lantern shone brighter, as if answering the question she hadn't yet asked. She took a breath, the air cool and salt-stained, and turned toward the hallway, her steps echoing on the stone floor.

The scene ends there, the mystery unresolved, the wind, the letter, and the map all lingering in the air like a promise yet to be fulfilled.

"Any letters for the lighthouse?" she asked, glancing at him.

"No, nothing today," Jack replied, his eyes following the path of her gaze to the letter.

"Thank you," she murmured, closing the door softly behind him. She retreated to the table and broke the seal, her hands trembling slightly as she unfolded the crisp paper inside.

The letter was written in a familiar, sloping hand, a hand she thought she had long forgotten. She read it once, then twice, her eyes darting back and forth as if each word might change, might disappear if she blinked.

_Dear Maggie,_

_If you are reading this, it means I have succeeded in my mission. I hope you are well and that life on the island has treated you kindly. I miss you, and I know this will come as a shock, but I am writing to you from beyond the grave._

_Since my death eleven years ago, I have been unable to rest, unable to move on. I have watched over you from afar, felt the cold touch of the sea that took me, and now I am free._

_So many things I wanted to say but could not when I was alive. I am sorry for leaving you in the midst of our greatest trial. It was never my intention to let you suffer alone._

_Since my departure, I have seen how you have transformed this place into something beautiful, and I am proud of how you've lived your life in my absence._

_Love always,_

_Ben_

Margaret's fingers tightened around the letter, crumpling the edges. The wind outside howled softly, as if echoing her own turmoil. She placed the letter down, her gaze fixed on the sea outside. The waves crashed against the rocky shore, their rhythm a constant reminder of her solitude.

She heard a faint sound, a whisper that seemed to float on the wind. She turned sharply, her heart pounding, but saw nothing out of the ordinary. The cottage was empty save for the shadows cast by the fading light of the setting sun.

The letter sat on the table, its message unresolved, unanswered. Margaret reached for a pen and a fresh piece of paper, her hand hovering over the page as if she feared what words might come out. She stared at the paper, the ink standing ready, but no words formed. The wind grew stronger, and she shivered, not from the cold but from an inexplicable sense of foreboding.

She folded the letter carefully, tucking it into a drawer beside the small stack of photographs, each one capturing a different memory of her brother. The drawer clicked shut, sealing away the mystery for another day.

As the night settled over the island, Margaret retired to her small bed, the weight of the letter heavy in her thoughts. The lighthouse’s beam cut through the darkness outside, a silent guardian through the long, quiet nights. She closed her eyes, the remnants of the day slowly fading, the mystery of the letter lingering, unsolved, in the stillness of the night.
